The Second Tier

Is it worth taking your best people off the frontier to bring everyone else along? Three organisations run here on an identical stream of ideas, differing only in treatment — so what separates the lines is the policy, not luck. The headline, which was not what I expected going in: being able to hand work down is worth far more than training people, and it is the finding that survives scrutiny. What counts as outputHirshleifer · Kremer

Weakest link: output is capped by your least-capable people (Kremer's O-ring, 1993) — a new workflow only works if everyone can use it. Best shot: output is set by your strongest (a research frontier). Average: in between. This choice does more to decide whether training pays than anything else on the page.

What this models, and what it doesn't

120 people in three roles — techies, analysts, managers — differing in how readily they adopt (Rogers' innovativeness), how much capacity to change they still hold, and how high their switching cost sits (Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009). Ideas arrive at the frontier; adopting one means you now own its support work, which slows you. You can hand that work to a colleague — but only one who already knows that technology, which is what makes the second tier's proximity matter. Training doesn't push anyone forward; it only lowers their switching cost, leaving the model to decide whether they actually move.

Read this before drawing conclusions — especially about training. The handoff result is robust: it holds at ~+28–32% across every integration step and population size tested. The training result is not. Re-running the identical model at different timesteps moved it from +58% to −1.4%, flipping sign — so it is substantially a numerical artifact, not a property of the organisation. This page runs at the finest timestep tested, where training's effect is close to nothing. Treat any training gain you see below as unproven. Two further limits: the production-function control largely determines the answer, so this can only show how the trade-off behaves under an assumption you supply; and handoff matters here only because it is gated on capability rather than proximity — gate it on proximity and the whole mechanism goes inert, because there is always somebody standing nearby.
A simplified illustrative model, not a validated theory of organisations — built alongside "Psychology of Novelty & Habit Disruption" in When Machines Changed the Job.
