The model, in brief

Each dot carries a habit strength and a true, personal fit with the new normal. A rollout jolts arousal by habit strength × disruption size — Shiffrin & Schneider's automaticity (1977): the more a workflow ran on autopilot, the harder the jolt when it's interrupted. The initial appraisal amplifies a bad fit by the loss-aversion multiplier — Mandler's split (1975): arousal and valence are computed independently, so the same interruption lands as elation for one person and distress for another. As arousal cools, that hot-state amplification fades too: appraisal relaxes toward the change's real fit, plus a small, fixed liking bonus from familiarity (Zajonc's mere-exposure effect) — enough to soften a mediocre change, not enough to rescue a genuinely bad one. So the population's end state depends on whether the change was actually good: Content if so, a lasting pocket of Bored or Distressed if not. Arousal isn't strictly one-and-done, either: each day, continued use has a small chance of surfacing something new — a hidden bug, an unexpected win. Mandler's arousal responds to any fresh discrepancy, not just the original rollout, so a calmed-down agent can spike back up well after the fact.

A simplified illustrative model, not a validated psychological instrument — built to accompany "Psychology of Novelty & Habit Disruption" in When Machines Changed the Job.
