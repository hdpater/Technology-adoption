The Innovator's Wake

Agents wander at random until an idea's pull is worth the effort of changing their mind. Occasionally one of them originates an idea near where they stand; a good one, or one enough others have already joined, is worth the effort — a bad one isn't, and dies where it started. Outside ideas occasionally arrive from off-stage, unearned by anyone here. Position is technology status: further right is more advanced. Companion to Surprise Circumplex — this one asks what happens across a population and over time, not what one person feels.

The model, in brief

Agents wander at random — Granovetter's threshold models of collective behavior (1978) are the frame: each agent only acts once the case for acting clears a personal threshold, here the effort cost of changing what you think, cheaper for the innovative and dearer for everyone else. Every idea carries a quality, fixed at birth; its pull is quality × a crowd term — Bass's diffusion model (1969) again, innovation and imitation — and an idea that never clears anyone's threshold just decays where it started, unadopted. Occasional outside ideas arrive unearned by anyone in this population, the way an adjacent field's breakthrough shows up in yours. The tension is the point: standing still is free; moving costs effort; only a good enough idea, or a big enough crowd already there, is worth paying it. Pull also fades with distance faster than it used to, and new ideas favor agents already near the front — so the frontier keeps advancing rather than idling in place, and falling behind has a real spatial cost, not just a timing one. The defaults below are tuned so roughly a fifth to a third of the population sits this out at any moment, while the most innovative agents stay tightly bunched just ahead of everyone else — a small vanguard the rest is chasing, not a lone rocket departing alone. None of this stays separated on its own, though: anyone can in principle still catch up, so left long enough the population just cycles back into one cluster. A career makes the separation permanent. Every agent's capacity to move erodes over time — faster the more they've actually chased ideas, since adapting itself is what wears you down — until it hits zero and they simply stop, visibly fading and shrinking as they wind down. A stopped agent is retired and replaced by a fresh recruit at the current cutting edge: full capacity, born already caught up. That's what keeps the frontier a frontier — not distance, but succession.

A simplified illustrative model, not a validated diffusion model — built to accompany "Psychology of Novelty & Habit Disruption" in When Machines Changed the Job.
