The Capability Gate

What actually creates a second tier capable of taking work off your frontier — and the data you would need to collect before spending anything on it.

Where this came from

A simulation of an organisation adopting a stream of new technologies produced one robust result and one failure. Being able to hand support work down was worth roughly 30% of organisational output, and held across every population size and integration step tested. Training budget was worth almost nothing — and an earlier build that showed a large positive effect turned out to be a numerical artifact, swinging from +58% to −1.4% on timestep alone.

The mechanism behind the robust half is worth stating precisely, because it is the whole argument: a handoff only succeeds when the receiver has already independently operated that specific technology. When the model instead allowed handoff to anyone standing nearby, the entire effect vanished — because in a working organisation there is always somebody standing nearby. Proximity is free. Capability is not.

The mechanism has a name

This is not a new idea, which is reassuring rather than disappointing. Cohen & Levinthal's absorptive capacity (1990) holds that an organisation's ability to take up new knowledge is a function of its prior related knowledge — and that this capacity accrues largely as a by-product of doing the work, not as a product of being told about it. You cannot absorb what you have no foundation in.

That is the capability gate, arrived at from the other direction. It explains why the training lever underperformed: training that does not culminate in someone independently owning the thing produces awareness, not absorptive capacity, and awareness cannot receive a handoff. It also reframes the policy question. The useful question is not "how much should we spend on training?" but "for each technology we depend on, how many people have independently operated it — and what is the cheapest way to make that number two?"

What produces an up-to-date second tier

Each attribute below is tagged by where it comes from, because they are not equally well-founded and you should weight them differently.

1. Coverage per technology, not average skill

from the model

The gate is per-technology and binary. An organisation of brilliant people who each solely own one system has zero handoff capacity, regardless of headcount, seniority or training spend. What matters is the count of independent operators per technology — the bus factor — not the mean capability of the staff. This is the single most actionable reframing here: it turns an unbounded investment question into a finite list of specific gaps.

2. Capability is earned by doing the work

literature

Absorptive capacity is cumulative and built through practice (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990). Shadowing, documentation-reading and briefings lower the cost of a later adoption but do not themselves create a receiver. The implication is uncomfortable: the only reliable way to produce a second owner is to let someone be an owner, with the mistakes that entails.

3. Slack in the second tier

from the modelliterature

In the simulation, saturated receivers refuse work, and handoff fails even where capability exists. This matches the standard queueing result that utilisation approaching capacity destroys throughput. The counterintuitive consequence: deliberately under-loading your second tier is a precondition for handoff, not a luxury. An organisation that runs everyone at full utilisation has, by construction, eliminated its own ability to shed work downward.

4. Rotation of new work, not just old work

judgement

If tier 1 always takes the new thing — because they are fastest, which they are — then tier 2 never passes the gate, and handoff capacity decays permanently. The real training budget is therefore an assignment policy: deliberately letting a slower person take a new technology first, and paying the short-term speed loss on purpose. This is the intervention the simulation suggests should work, and it is not what most organisations mean by "training".

5. Low switching costs, and no status penalty for not knowing

literature

Resistance to new systems is driven principally by switching costs and by perceived threat to one's standing and competence (Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009). Where admitting unfamiliarity is expensive, adoption stalls regardless of budget — and the people who most need to pass the gate are the ones with most to lose by visibly starting as beginners.

6. Codification lowers the cost of passing the gate

judgement

Not in the model. Converting tacit ownership into written, runnable, testable form should reduce what it costs a second person to reach independent operation. If true, well-codified organisations should show materially shorter handoff latency at the same bus factor — which is a testable prediction rather than an assertion, and one worth checking before investing in documentation as a substitute for practice.

7. Hiring as a substitute for internal capability-building

from the model

People arrive already fluent in current external practice, which refreshes the frontier without spending frontier time. But it has a specific failure mode: new hires know the industry's current technology, not your systems, so hiring refreshes tier 1 while doing nothing for coverage of your own legacy. An organisation that meets every capability gap by hiring will find its bus-factor-one list growing regardless.

8. Coupling — whether people watch each other

from the modelliterature

In the simulation, capability investment paid noticeably more in tightly-coupled organisations, where people adopt partly because peers did (the imitation term in Bass's diffusion model, 1969), and paid least where everyone decided alone. Communities of practice are therefore a multiplier on any other investment here rather than an alternative to them.

What to collect

None of this requires measuring "skill", which is fortunate, because skill is hard to measure and easy to argue about. Every construct below has a concrete operational proxy that most organisations already emit as exhaust.

What you're measuringWhere it already existsThe decision it informs
Independent operators per technology On-call rota history; commit and review authorship; deployment logs; who resolved the ticket, not who was assigned it The bus-factor-one list. This is the core dataset — everything else is supporting.
Frontier support load Pages and interrupts per senior person; ticket assignment; calendar fragmentation Whether handoff is even your constraint. If seniors already spend little on support, none of this matters.
Handoff latency Time from a technology's introduction to the first incident resolved by someone other than whoever introduced it Whether the gate is passable in practice, and how long passing it takes.
Second-tier utilisation Work-in-progress counts, queue lengths, cycle times Whether slack — not capability — is what's blocking handoff.
Adoption timing per person per technology First independent use, from the same sources as coverage Your internal diffusion curve; lets you estimate how much people follow peers versus decide alone.
Time to independence Onboarding date to first unsupervised contribution on a given technology Your switching cost in real units — the thing training is supposed to reduce.
What actually caps delivery Whether delivery times track your slowest contributor or your fastest Your production function. This governs the sign of the whole answer and cannot be assumed.

Turning that into a policy

  1. Rank technologies by frontier time consumed × inverse bus factor. Things that are both single-owner and expensive in senior attention are your bottleneck list, and it will be shorter than you expect.
  2. For each, pick the cheapest route to a second independent operator — rotating the next piece of work on it, pairing through a real change, codifying enough that someone else can run it, or hiring for it. These have very different costs and the answer differs per technology.
  3. Check second-tier slack before starting. If utilisation is already at capacity, creating capability will not produce handoff, and you will conclude — wrongly — that capability-building doesn't work.
  4. Establish your production function before arguing about budget, because it determines whether bringing up the tail shows in output at all.

How you would know this is wrong

Stated as checks rather than claims, because a toy model earns very little deference:

  • The gate itself: do people who were trained on a technology but never independently operated it absorb handoffs as readily as people who did operate it? If yes, the capability gate is wrong and conventional training should work fine.
  • Relevance: if your senior people already spend little time on support for things they introduced, handoff is not your constraint and this entire framing is beside the point.
  • Codification as substitute: if handoff latency is short despite bus factor one, something — most likely codification — is substituting for practice, and attribute 2 is overstated.
  • Aggregation: if delivery tracks your fastest contributors, improvements to the tail will not appear in output no matter how real they are.

What this cannot tell you

Do not read this as evidence against training

The simulation showed that the training effect was not robust, which is a different and much weaker statement than showing training doesn't work. A toy model failing to detect an effect is poor evidence that the effect is absent — particularly here, where the training mechanism was the crudest thing in the model. The defensible claim is narrow: handoff capability is worth measuring and investing in first, because its effect was large and survived scrutiny that the training effect did not.

Three further limits

The production function is an input to this analysis, not an output of it — the model will happily tell you whatever your assumption implies. The simulated organisation's laggard tail is thin, so weakest-link conclusions were the noisiest and least trustworthy. And every attribute above tagged judgement is reasoning by analogy from a simplified model to real organisations, which is exactly the step where this sort of work usually goes wrong.
